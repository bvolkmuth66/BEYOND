Resilience Isn't Toughness

Sometimes strength is enduring. Sometimes it is knowing what needs to change so we can keep going.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Resilience

For a long time, I think I confused resilience with toughness.

Keep going.

Don't complain.

Figure it out.

Carry your share, and if necessary, carry a little more.

There are times when that mindset has served me well. Sometimes life doesn't pause while we collect ourselves. Something needs to be done, people are depending on us, and the only reasonable choice is to keep moving.

I've done that.

But I've also learned there is a problem with treating endurance as the only evidence of resilience.

Eventually, being able to take more isn't necessarily the same as being able to recover.

Toughness has its place

I don't want to dismiss toughness.

There have been moments in my life when quitting wasn't much of an option. The Marine Corps certainly taught me something about functioning while tired, uncomfortable, and wishing circumstances were different.

Parenthood has taught me that too. So has work. So has life.

Sometimes resilience really does look like putting your head down and taking another step.

But that can't be the entire definition.

Because if the only response I have to difficulty is endure more, eventually I'm not adapting.

I'm just absorbing damage.

Resilience isn't only about how much we can take. It's also about how well we recover, adapt, and return.

Repair isn't weakness

We understand this almost instinctively with physical things.

When something important begins to wear out, we maintain it. We reinforce the weak point. We repair what's torn. We replace what no longer works.

We don't praise a machine because it destroyed itself rather than stopping for maintenance.

People aren't machines, of course.

But somehow I've occasionally expected more from myself than I'd expect from almost anything else.

Rest can feel like falling behind. Asking for help can feel like admitting I couldn't handle something. Changing how I do something can feel suspiciously close to giving up.

But what if those aren't failures of resilience?

What if they're part of it?

The question isn't only “Can I keep going?”

What will it cost me to keep going this way?

What needs repair?

What could I change?

Where do I need help?

What would recovery actually require?

Those questions don't always lead to stopping.

Sometimes the answer really is: Take another step.

But sometimes the resilient decision is to rest. Or adjust the load. Or ask someone to carry part of it. Or admit that the way I've been doing something isn't sustainable.

That feels much closer to resilience than endurance alone.

Come back differently

I used to think resilience meant returning to exactly who I was before something difficult happened.

I'm less convinced of that now.

Some experiences change us.

Some should.

The goal doesn't have to be returning unchanged.

Maybe resilience means taking what happened seriously enough to let it teach us something.

We recover what we can. We repair what needs attention. We accept help when we need it. We change what isn't working.

And then we return.

Maybe a little different.

Hopefully a little wiser.

Still moving.

There is strength in enduring something difficult.

But there is also strength in recognizing when endurance alone isn't enough.

Resilience isn't proving that nothing can break us. It's learning how to repair, adapt, and keep living when something does.
