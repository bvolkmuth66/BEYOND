The Limit You Feel Isn't Always the Limit You Have

Sometimes the voice telling us to stop isn't warning us that we can't continue. It's simply asking us to return to somewhere comfortable.

Brian Volkmuth  •  4 min read  •  Resilience

During almost every difficult thing, a little voice starts negotiating with me.

That's enough.

You've done more than most people would.

There's no shame in stopping.

Sometimes that voice is right. There are real limits. Bodies get injured. Resources run out. Circumstances change. Wisdom includes knowing when continuing would be foolish.

But sometimes I am not anywhere near my limit.

I am just uncomfortable.

And those two things can feel remarkably similar.

The limit I was sure was there

I learned some of this a long time ago in the Marine Corps.

I remember being tired, hungry, uncomfortable, carrying more than I wanted to carry, and feeling certain I had reached the point where I had little left to give. Then we would find out there were still several miles to go.

And somehow, I walked them.

That is the part that stayed with me. Not the miles themselves. Not proving how tough I was. It was realizing that the limit I had been certain was there was not actually there.

My mind had drawn a line before my ability had.

I have met versions of that line many times since then: raising a family, going back to school, accepting leadership responsibilities, and learning difficult things.

I have convinced myself that I was not ready, qualified, or capable enough to take the next step. I have let that belief paralyze me when a decision had to be made. Looking back, some of those limits were not real.

I had treated an assumption about myself as though it were a fact.

The circumstances changed, but the conversation in my head did not change much.

I don't know if I can do this.

I have come to believe that is not necessarily a reason to stop. Sometimes that is where the interesting part begins.

We rarely discover our capabilities by doing only the things we already know we can do.

Discomfort is not the same as danger

I have avoided difficult classes, uncomfortable conversations, unfamiliar rooms, and opportunities I was not sure I could handle. Each can put me beyond comfortable but still well short of impossible.

That is an important place to spend some time.

But not every limit should be challenged. Pain can warn me that I am injured. Exhaustion can tell me I need rest. Responsibilities to other people constrain some choices. Sometimes stopping, changing direction, or saying no is the wiser decision.

So when I feel myself pulling back, I am trying to ask better questions.

Is continuing likely to harm me or someone else, or is it simply going to make me uncomfortable?

Is there something I genuinely lack, or something I could learn?

Am I responding to evidence, or to fear about what might happen?

And perhaps the hardest one: If I stop here, am I protecting something important, or am I protecting myself from uncertainty?

Those questions do not guarantee the right answer. They are not supposed to. But they make it harder for me to mistake fear for wisdom.

Some boundaries protect us. They deserve respect. Others mostly protect our comfort.

I am trying to get better at knowing which one I am standing in front of.

Maybe “I can't” deserves a question mark

When I catch myself thinking, I can't do that, I am trying to change the period to a question mark.

I can't do that?

Why not?

Maybe there is a legitimate answer. Or maybe I am afraid of failing, looking foolish, or discovering that I am not as capable as I hoped.

Those fears deserve examination, not automatic obedience.

Failure does not necessarily prove I have found my limit. Sometimes it tells me my current preparation, approach, knowledge, or experience is not sufficient yet.

That is not a verdict.

That is information.

Go find out

I do not believe I can do anything simply because I believe in myself. I have real limitations.

What I question is how often I have actually found them.

How many boundaries in my life have I tested, and how many have I simply accepted?

I can push a little farther when it makes sense. Take the class. Have the conversation. Apply for the opportunity. Try the thing I might fail at.

Not recklessly. Not because success is guaranteed. And not because stopping is weakness.

Sometimes the only way to discover what I am capable of is to venture beyond what I already know.

Maybe the goal is not to ignore my limits.

Maybe it is to make sure they are actually mine.

You deserve to know whether the limit you have been living within is actually yours.
