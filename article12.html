You Are Not Too Old to Become Someone New

Getting older may change how we grow. It doesn't mean we're finished becoming.

Brian Volkmuth  •  4 min read  •  Growth

When I was younger, I had some ideas about what my life might look like.

This wasn't it.

I don't mean that negatively. I mean I couldn't have described the person I would eventually become because some of those versions of me didn't exist yet.

At 18, I joined the Marine Corps. College didn't seem like a realistic path for me then.

I became a husband and father.

Our family grew.

Later, Alicia and I became foster parents. That opened another chapter of our lives, one that eventually led to adopting two amazing kids and expanding what family meant to us.

At 42, I walked into a college classroom wondering whether I belonged there.

Eventually, I became the person standing at the front of that classroom.

I didn't see this version of myself coming

I became an educator. I continued my education. I took on leadership responsibilities I hadn't imagined having. And after becoming the instructor, I eventually went back to being the student again.

When I look at that progression, what strikes me isn't how much I've accomplished.

It's how impossible it would have been for a younger version of me to predict the person I would become.

Some of the most important versions of ourselves aren't planned. We meet them because life gives us an opportunity to become them.

There have been several points in my life when I could have looked around and said:

This is probably who I am now.

Maybe that's one of the quiet dangers of getting older. Not age itself, but becoming so accustomed to the person we've become that we stop leaving room for whoever might come next.

Becoming doesn't require leaving ourselves behind

I don't want to reinvent myself every few years.

There are parts of me I hope never change.

I'm a husband, father, grandfather, Marine, educator, and student. Every one of those identities carries people, experiences, responsibilities, successes, mistakes, and lessons with it.

They accumulate.

Becoming foster parents didn't erase the family Alicia and I already had. It asked us to expand it.

Adopting our two kids didn't replace one version of our family with another. It made our understanding of family larger.

Going to college didn't erase the Marine who hadn't thought college was achievable.

The Marine became a student.

The student became an educator.

The educator became a leader.

And the leader went back to being a student again.

None of those required abandoning what came before.

They added to it.

We don't start over from nothing. We start again with everything life has already taught us.

Beginner is still available

That is why I don't want to reach an age where being a beginner embarrasses me.

Being new at something can be uncomfortable.

Someone younger may know far more than I do. I may ask basic questions. I may misunderstand something obvious. I may be terrible at it for a while.

Good.

There are worse things than being inexperienced.

One of them might be becoming so protective of the things we're already good at that we stop discovering anything else about ourselves.

I don't need to know where every new beginning leads.

I just don't want age to become my excuse for refusing to begin.

I hope I'm not finished

I don't know exactly who I'll be ten years from now.

I hope I don't.

Because if I can already describe that person completely, I've probably left myself too little room to become someone I haven't met yet.

I've already met several versions of myself that a younger me couldn't have predicted.

I'd like to leave room for at least a few more.

Maybe that's one of the gifts of getting older.

We finally have enough history to see that who we have been has never completely determined who we have to become.

Age changes us. Experience changes us. Responsibilities change us.

But none of those automatically closes the door.

Sometimes they give us more to walk through it with.

We're not done becoming.
Not yet.
