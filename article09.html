I'm Not Extraordinary. I Just Tried.

Sometimes calling someone else extraordinary is another way of convincing ourselves we could never do what they did.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Resilience

I've never been particularly comfortable when someone tells me they couldn't have done something I've done.

I appreciate what they're trying to say. Usually it's meant as a compliment.

I don't know how you did that.

I could never have handled that.

You must be incredibly strong.

The strange part is that the person they're describing doesn't feel much like me.

I don't consider myself extraordinary.

I'm an average man with average skills who has sometimes found himself in situations where something needed to be done.

So I tried.

The view from the outside

From the outside, difficult experiences can look different than they felt while we were living them.

Other people see the whole story.

We remember Tuesday afternoon. The problem that needed solving. The phone call that had to be made. The person who needed us. The deadline that wasn't moving. The next decision.

Most difficult things I've been through weren't handled by some heroic version of myself who appeared precisely when needed.

I was still me.

I got tired. I doubted myself. I made mistakes. Sometimes I wanted circumstances to be different.

Then I dealt with whatever came next.

Something doesn't become possible only when an extraordinary person shows up. Sometimes an ordinary person simply starts doing what needs to be done.

Be careful who you make extraordinary

There's something else that bothers me about hearing: I could never do that.

I'm not convinced that's true.

When we decide someone accomplished something because that person possesses unusual strength, intelligence, courage, or resilience, we may unintentionally give ourselves an excuse.

They're different from me.

They can handle things I can't.

Maybe.

People absolutely have different abilities, resources, experiences, and circumstances. Pretending otherwise wouldn't be honest.

But I think we underestimate how much capability gets built during the experience rather than before it.

We figure things out. We ask questions. We accept help. We make mistakes. We do tomorrow what yesterday taught us to do better.

And eventually someone looks at the whole thing and sees an accomplishment.

We remember learning it one piece at a time.

You don't have to feel extraordinary

This may be the part I want people to understand most.

You don't need to see yourself as unusually capable before attempting something difficult.

You don't need a heroic personality.

You don't need to become fearless.

Sometimes you need a reason to begin and enough willingness to take the next reasonable step.

None of those steps looks especially extraordinary by itself.

That's probably why we miss them when we're looking at someone else's story.

We see the finished distance instead of the individual steps.

Maybe you're underselling yourself

I don't know what another person can endure.

I don't know what someone will be capable of when circumstances change.

Sometimes I don't even know that about myself.

That's why I could never do that deserves some caution.

Maybe you couldn't.

There are real limits. We've talked about those throughout BEYOND.

But maybe you've never been asked. Maybe you've never had the reason. Maybe you've never taken the first step that teaches you how to take the second.

Don't make me extraordinary just so you can make yourself incapable.

I tried. I learned. I failed at parts of it. I accepted help. I kept going when continuing made sense.

That's not a particularly heroic story.

It may be something better.

It may be an ordinary one.

And ordinary people may be capable of far more than we give them credit for.
