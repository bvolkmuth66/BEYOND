Courage Doesn't Require Confidence

Sometimes courage begins before confidence ever shows up.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Courage

I've spent a surprising amount of my life waiting to feel ready.

Ready to speak. Ready to lead. Ready to take responsibility for something that mattered. Ready to make a decision when other people might be affected by the outcome.

I used to think confidence was supposed to come first. That before stepping forward, I should reach some point where the doubt disappeared and I could tell myself:

I've got this.

Sometimes that happens.

A lot of the time, it doesn't.

Confidence and courage aren't the same thing

Confidence usually comes from evidence.

I've done this before. I've practiced. I've succeeded. I understand what will probably happen next.

That kind of confidence is useful. I've earned it in parts of my life, and I value it.

But new situations don't always offer that evidence.

There have been times when I've been asked to lead before I felt like a leader. I've stood in front of a room knowing people expected me to know what I was doing while part of me was still wondering whether I actually did.

The responsibility was real.

So was the doubt.

And sometimes I had to step forward anyway.

Confidence says, “I can do this.” Courage sometimes says, “I don't know if I can do this, but I'm going to step forward anyway.”

That difference matters.

Waiting to feel different

There are moments when hesitation is telling me something useful.

Maybe I haven't prepared enough. Maybe I don't understand the situation. Maybe I'm missing a skill, information, or experience that genuinely matters.

Those are problems I can work on.

But sometimes I've done the preparation. I've thought through the consequences. I've asked questions. I've learned what I can.

And I'm still nervous.

Do I need more preparation, or am I simply waiting to feel different before I begin?

That's an uncomfortable question because feelings don't always cooperate with our schedules.

Responsibility can arrive before confidence. Opportunity can arrive before confidence. The moment to speak can arrive before confidence.

Sometimes there isn't time to become the version of ourselves we imagine would handle the situation perfectly.

We have to work with the person who actually showed up.

Courage can be quiet

I think we sometimes make courage too dramatic.

We imagine enormous risks, heroic acts, or moments when someone conquers fear and charges forward.

Most of the courage I've seen in ordinary life looks smaller than that.

It's raising your hand. Having the conversation you've been avoiding. Admitting you were wrong. Asking for help. Walking into the room. Accepting responsibility. Saying something when remaining silent would be easier. Trying again after getting it wrong.

None of those actions requires us to suddenly feel fearless.

Maybe that's the point.

Courage doesn't require the doubt to leave. It requires us to decide how much authority we're going to give it.

Let confidence catch up

Something interesting happens after we act.

The unfamiliar becomes experience. The first attempt becomes something we've done once. Then twice.

Eventually, some of the uncertainty that seemed so large at the beginning gets replaced by evidence.

We learn what worked. We learn what didn't. We discover what we know and what we still need to learn.

And sometimes confidence finally arrives.

Just later than we expected.

I've begun to trust that sequence more.

Not every time. Not every situation.

But often enough that I no longer believe I need confidence's permission before I move.

I still listen to doubt. Sometimes doubt is pointing toward something important.

I just don't automatically let it make the decision.

Courage isn't the absence of doubt. Sometimes it's simply deciding that doubt doesn't get the final vote.
