Small Hard Things Prepare Us for Big Hard Things

Sometimes growth isn't one big leap outside our comfort zone. It's choosing a small step outside it often enough that the boundary eventually moves.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Growth

When I started college at 42, I knew returning to a classroom wasn't going to be entirely comfortable.

As part of applying for VA Chapter 31 Vocational Rehabilitation, I took the Myers-Briggs assessment. The result was INTJ, with a score of 30 on the introversion side.

I remember looking at that result and thinking:

I can't afford to be that introverted if I'm going to be successful in college.

I wasn't trying to become someone else. But I believed I needed to become more comfortable interacting with instructors and other students.

So I made one very small decision.

I changed where I sat

I did some research about classroom interaction and decided I would sit in the front-right side of the classroom, from my perspective facing the instructor.

My thinking was simple: if I sat where the instructor was more likely to interact with me, it would be harder for me to disappear into the room.

I'd be seen. Participation would be easier. Avoiding interaction would require a little more effort.

It wasn't a dramatic act of courage.

I just chose a seat that made hiding a little harder.

Then I kept choosing it. Class after class.

Small enough to repeat

I didn't decide that I was suddenly going to become outgoing. I practiced one behavior that didn't come naturally to me.

Then I practiced it again. And again.

The discomfort was manageable. That mattered.

If I'd tried to remake myself overnight, I probably would have failed. Instead, I gave myself a small challenge I could repeat often enough for it to become less challenging.

Sometimes growth isn't one big leap outside our comfort zone. It's choosing a small step outside it often enough that the boundary eventually moves.

I haven't taken the Myers-Briggs assessment in probably twenty years, so I couldn't tell you what four letters it would assign me today.

But I know I'm not the same person who walked into college at 42.

Practice before you need it

I think small difficulties matter because we don't get to choose every difficult thing that enters our lives.

Some challenges simply arrive: loss, responsibility, change, failure, or someone needing us when we don't feel particularly capable of being needed.

We don't get to schedule those moments after we've developed exactly the skills we're going to need.

But we can practice some of those skills beforehand.

Being uncomfortable. Speaking when silence would be easier. Asking questions. Trying something unfamiliar. Staying with frustration. Asking for help.

No individual repetition looks especially important.

That's partly the point.

Don't manufacture suffering

Growth doesn't require making life unnecessarily miserable.

Harder isn't automatically better.

Is this manageable difficulty helping me build something I may need later?

The seat I chose in that classroom wasn't punishment.

It was practice.

Eventually, something I once had to choose deliberately became much more natural.

Move the boundary

Twenty years later, I don't think much about where I sit because I'm afraid an instructor might interact with me.

Somewhere along the way, that particular boundary moved.

Not because of one enormous act.

Because of a small decision, repeated.

I suspect more of our growth happens that way than we realize.

One conversation. One class. One question. One uncomfortable seat.

Then again. And again.

Until what once required deliberate effort becomes something we simply know how to do.

Small hard things don't prepare us for every big hard thing. But they give us a chance to practice becoming someone who can face them.
