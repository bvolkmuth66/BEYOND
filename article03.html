Do Something You're Not Sure You Can Do

Sometimes the point isn't knowing that you can. It's being willing to find out.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Growth

Some of the more important decisions in my life started with an uncomfortable admission:

I'm not sure I can do this.

I'd like to say confidence followed quickly.

Usually, it didn't.

There was preparation. There was uncertainty. And there was that persistent question:

What if I can't?

I've come to believe that question isn't always a warning.

Sometimes it's an invitation.

Certainty is a pretty high price of admission

There are plenty of things I know I can do. I've done them before. I understand the expectations and can reasonably predict the outcome.

There's comfort in that.

But if I require that same certainty before attempting something new, I've created a problem.

How can I know I'm capable of something I've never done?

Going back to school required a first attempt. So did taking on leadership responsibilities, teaching something new, and stepping into situations where I wasn't the person with all the answers.

There have been plenty of moments when I couldn't honestly say:

Yes, I know I can do this.

Sometimes the best I had was:

I think I can figure this out.

It turns out that can be enough.

If I only attempt things I already know I can do, I'll only ever confirm what I already know about myself.

Uncertainty isn't the same as being unprepared

I don't think uncertainty is a reason to ignore preparation.

Before I take on something difficult, I want to understand what I can. I want to know the consequences, recognize what I don't know, and decide whether I can learn what I'm missing.

This isn't about closing my eyes and jumping.

It's about recognizing that preparation and certainty aren't the same thing.

I can prepare thoroughly and still not know how something will turn out. I can have the necessary skills and still wonder whether they're enough. I can make a responsible decision and still be nervous about making it.

Eventually, waiting for uncertainty to disappear can stop being caution and become avoidance.

What happens if I try?

I'm trying to replace one question with another.

Instead of:

What if I can't do it?

I ask:

What happens if I try?

Maybe I succeed. Maybe I struggle. Maybe I fail. Maybe I discover that I'm missing knowledge, skill, or experience I didn't realize I needed.

There's information in every one of those outcomes.

The only outcome that guarantees I learn nothing about my ability is never making the attempt.

Is the risk genuinely too high, or is the biggest thing standing between me and this opportunity simply that I've never done it before?

That doesn't mean every opportunity deserves a yes. Some risks aren't worth taking. Responsibilities matter. The consequences of failure can matter enormously.

But sometimes the biggest thing standing between me and an opportunity is simply that I've never done it before.

That's not much of an argument against doing it.

Leave room to surprise yourself

I've spent enough years discovering that my first estimate of myself isn't always particularly accurate.

I've overestimated myself. That's humbling.

But I've underestimated myself too.

We can look at something unfamiliar and compare who we are today with the person we'd need to be after doing it successfully.

What we forget is everything that happens between those two points.

We learn. We adjust. We ask for help. We make mistakes. We get better.

Eventually, something that once felt uncertain becomes something we know how to do.

That's how our understanding of ourselves gets larger.

Not because someone convinced us anything was possible.

Because we were willing to try something we weren't certain we could do.

You don't have to know that you can. Sometimes you only need enough reason to find out.
