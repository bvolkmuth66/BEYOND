Failure Is Data

Failure can hurt without being useless. Sometimes the most valuable question is what it is trying to tell us.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Growth

I don't like failing.

I doubt many people do.

Even when I tell myself failure is part of learning, it can still feel personal. I can know intellectually that something didn't work and somehow translate that into:

Maybe I'm not good enough to do this.

Those aren't the same statement.

It has taken me a long time to get better at separating them.

The result and the judgment

When something fails, two things can happen almost immediately.

First, there is the result:

This didn't work.

Then comes the judgment:

I couldn't do it.

Or worse:

I'm not capable of doing it.

The result may be completely accurate. The judgment is something I've added.

I've seen this in school, work, leadership, relationships, and things I've tried to learn. Something doesn't go the way I expected, and disappointment wants to turn one unsuccessful attempt into a conclusion about me.

But failure doesn't actually tell me all of that.

The way I approached this, with what I knew and what I had available at the time, didn't produce the result I wanted.

That's information.

Failure becomes much more useful when I stop asking what it says about me and start asking what it says about the attempt.

Don't waste the failure

If something matters enough to be disappointed about, I want to get something from getting it wrong.

So I'm trying to ask more specific questions.

What actually happened?

Was I prepared?

Did I misunderstand the problem or use the wrong approach?

Was the goal realistic?

Did something happen that I simply couldn't control?

Those questions lead somewhere.

I'm a failure doesn't.

Once I've decided the failure is me, there isn't much left to examine.

But if the failure is an outcome, I can study it.

Not every failure means try again

Learning from failure doesn't mean repeating the same attempt indefinitely.

Sometimes the data says:

Prepare more.

Change the approach.

Ask for help.

Try again.

And sometimes the honest answer is:

Stop. This isn't working, and continuing doesn't make sense.

That isn't necessarily giving up.

If failure is information, I have to be willing to accept the information even when it tells me something I didn't want to hear.

The goal isn't endless persistence.

The goal is a better next decision.

Keep the pieces

I wish every failure came with an obvious lesson attached.

They don't.

Sometimes it takes time before I understand what happened. Sometimes the lesson I thought I'd learned changes after I have more experience.

And some failures simply hurt.

I don't think we need to pretend otherwise to make them useful.

But I also don't want to throw away everything an unsuccessful attempt can teach me simply because I wish it had turned out differently.

A failed attempt can expose a weak assumption. It can reveal where preparation was thin. It can show me a skill I need to develop. It can force me to reconsider the goal. It can even show me that I was closer than I thought.

None of that guarantees that the next attempt succeeds.

But it means the next decision doesn't have to begin from the same place.

Failure is not automatically wisdom. We turn it into wisdom by being willing to examine what happened.

And maybe that's enough.

The attempt may have failed. That doesn't mean the information has to go to waste.
