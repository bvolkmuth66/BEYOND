Sometimes Resilience Means Changing Direction

Changing the plan isn't always giving up. Sometimes it's what allows us to keep moving.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Resilience

I've stayed with things longer than I should have.

Partly because I don't like quitting.

Partly because persistence is something I value.

And partly because once I've invested enough time, effort, or pride into a direction, changing course can feel suspiciously like admitting I was wrong.

Sometimes I was wrong.

That's not necessarily the worst thing that can happen.

Continuing down the wrong path simply because I've already traveled a long way on it may be worse.

Persistence can become its own trap

There is a lot to admire about persistence.

Difficult things often require us to keep going after the excitement wears off, after progress slows, and after the work becomes inconvenient.

I've benefited from that kind of persistence.

But there's a difference between:

This is difficult.

and:

This isn't working.

Those can feel similar from inside the experience.

If I value perseverance too much, I can treat every obstacle as something that must be overcome rather than information I should consider.

Sometimes the obstacle really does mean: Work harder.

Sometimes it means: Find another way.

And occasionally it means: You shouldn't be going this direction anymore.

Persistence is valuable, but it isn't a substitute for paying attention.

The destination and the route

I think one reason changing direction is difficult is that we confuse the route with the destination.

Suppose something matters deeply to me. I build a plan for reaching it. Then the plan fails.

It's easy to conclude that the goal failed with it.

But those aren't necessarily the same thing.

The route I chose may have depended on circumstances that changed. I may have learned something I didn't know when I started. My responsibilities may be different now. I may simply have discovered a better way.

Changing the route doesn't automatically mean abandoning what mattered about the destination.

That distinction gives me more room to adapt without pretending the original effort was wasted.

Ask what you're actually protecting

Am I protecting the goal, or am I protecting the plan?

Am I continuing because this still makes sense, or because I've already invested so much in it?

If I were making this decision today, knowing what I know now, would I choose the same direction?

That last question can be uncomfortable.

It also cuts through a lot of history.

The time I've already spent matters.

But it doesn't get to make every future decision for me.

Changing direction doesn't erase the distance already traveled.

It uses what that distance taught us.

Keep moving, differently

I don't want resilience to become another word for stubbornness.

Sometimes resilience means enduring.

Sometimes it means recovering.

And sometimes it means looking at the path ahead, admitting that it no longer takes us where we need to go, and choosing another one.

That can require its own kind of courage.

There may be explanations to give. Expectations to change. Plans to abandon. Pride to swallow.

But there can also be relief in finally stopping the effort to make the wrong route work.

The important question isn't always: Can I keep going?

Sometimes it's:

Where am I actually trying to go?

Then we choose the route from where we are now.

Not from where we thought we'd be. Not from the plan we made years ago.
