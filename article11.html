Someone May Be Borrowing Your Belief in Them

Sometimes we believe in ourselves only after someone else does it first.

Brian Volkmuth  •  3 min read  •  Belief

When I walked into a college classroom at 42, I was surrounded by students who seemed young enough to be my kids.

It didn't take long before I started wondering whether I'd made a mistake.

Maybe I was in the wrong class.

Maybe I didn't belong there.

Maybe college simply wasn't something I was capable of doing.

The interesting thing is that those thoughts weren't new.

I'd had versions of them at 18.

My academic career to that point had been less than stellar. I knew I needed to be challenged, and I wanted something demanding from my life. I just didn't believe a college degree was something I could realistically achieve.

So I chose a different challenge.

I joined the Marine Corps.

More than two decades later, when I finally walked back into a classroom, some of those old assumptions were still waiting for me.

Someone else sometimes sees it first

At 42, I hadn't accumulated much evidence that I belonged in college.

I had plenty of life experience. I had raised a family, served in the Marines, worked, solved problems, and carried responsibilities.

But none of that completely silenced the question:

Can I actually do this?

What mattered during that period were people who treated the answer as though it might be yes before I was entirely convinced myself.

An instructor who expected me to figure something out. Someone who treated my questions as evidence that I was learning rather than evidence that I didn't belong. Someone who gave me responsibility instead of protecting me from it.

Those interactions may not have seemed remarkable to the people offering them.

They mattered to me.

Sometimes we borrow someone else's belief in us until we've accumulated enough evidence to begin believing in ourselves.

Eventually, I started collecting some of that evidence.

One problem solved. One class completed. One semester finished. Then another.

The borrowed belief gradually became mine.

Now I recognize the student

Today, I'm the instructor.

And every once in a while, I recognize the person sitting in front of me.

The student who is struggling with something everyone else seems to understand. The student who interprets one failed assignment as evidence that they don't belong. The student quietly wondering whether everyone else received some instruction manual they somehow missed.

I've been that student.

That changes how I think about helping.

If a student is struggling with a technical problem and I immediately solve it, I've demonstrated that I can solve it.

That's not what the student needs to discover.

Sometimes helping means asking a question. Pointing toward something they overlooked. Letting them struggle a little longer.

Saying: You're closer than you think.

Then stepping back.

Give them something real to borrow

Encouragement matters more when it's specific.

Not: You can do anything.

But:

You haven't quit even though this has been difficult.

Look at what you changed after the first attempt.

You know more now than you did an hour ago.

I wouldn't be asking you to do this if I didn't believe you could learn it.

Those statements give someone evidence.

The goal isn't to make someone dependent on our confidence.

It's to give them enough of it to continue until experience begins supplying confidence of their own.

I know that seat

At 42, I didn't need someone to convince me I was extraordinary.

I needed enough reason to believe that maybe I belonged in the room.

Eventually, I proved the rest to myself.

Now, when I see someone else sitting there wondering whether they belong, part of me recognizes the place immediately.

I know that seat.

And sometimes that may be exactly why we're in a position to help someone else out of it.

Someone may be borrowing your belief in them. Give them something honest to carry until they can build their own.
