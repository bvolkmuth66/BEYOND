Comfort Can Quietly Become a Cage

There is nothing wrong with being comfortable. The problem begins when comfort starts making our decisions for us.

Brian Volkmuth  •  4 min read  •  Growth

I like comfort.

I think that's worth admitting at the beginning.

I like knowing what I'm doing. I like familiar routines. I like being around people I know. I like having some idea how things are going to turn out.

After enough years of uncertainty, responsibility, work, and people depending on you, there's something pretty appealing about reaching a place where things feel settled.

And there is absolutely nothing wrong with that.

The problem is that I've sometimes confused being comfortable with being where I belong.

Those aren't necessarily the same thing.

Comfort rarely looks like a cage

When I think about the decisions that have changed my life, many of them required leaving something comfortable behind.

Going back to school. Taking on responsibilities I wasn't certain I was ready for. Learning something unfamiliar when I was already competent at what I knew. Walking into situations where I wasn't the person with all the answers.

None of those choices felt particularly comfortable at first.

That's probably why comfort can be so convincing.

It doesn't stand in front of us like an obstacle. It feels more like a perfectly reasonable argument for staying where we are.

Things are fine.

Why make this harder?

You don't need to prove anything.

Maybe later.

Those aren't always bad arguments. Sometimes they're exactly right.

But I've also used them when the more honest answer was:

I don't want to be uncomfortable again.
Comfort doesn't have to stop us. Sometimes it only has to make staying where we are feel reasonable enough.

Some comfort is earned

I don't want this to become another version of get outside your comfort zone. I've never particularly liked that phrase.

There are things in my life I have worked hard to make comfortable.

Home should feel comfortable. Relationships built over decades should have familiarity in them. There should be times when I can sit down, stop pushing, and appreciate where I am without immediately asking what challenge comes next.

Rest matters. Stability matters. Contentment matters.

So the question isn't:

Am I comfortable?

I'm starting to think the better question is:

What is this comfort doing for me?

Is it restoring me so I can step back into difficult things?

Or is it giving me permission to avoid them?

Is this stability supporting a life I've intentionally chosen?

Or have I stopped choosing because changing anything would be inconvenient, uncertain, or frightening?

That's harder to answer.

Restoring or restricting?

I've started thinking of comfort in two very different ways.

What is this comfort doing for me?
Restoring Me

Rest, reflection, recovery, connection, and enough space to regain the energy to engage with life again.

Restricting Me

Avoiding the unfamiliar, delaying decisions, declining opportunities, and steadily finding more reasons not to leave what is known.

Comfort that restores me gives something back. It lets me rest, think, recover, spend time with people who matter to me, and regain the energy to engage with life again.

Comfort that restricts me slowly asks less of me. It encourages me to avoid the unfamiliar conversation. Not apply for the opportunity. Not learn the difficult thing. Not take the risk.

When I leave this comfortable place, do I feel restored and ready to engage again, or do I increasingly look for reasons not to leave it?

That's a distinction I think I understand better now than I did when I was younger.

Maybe maturity isn't about constantly pushing ourselves.

Maybe it's about becoming more honest about why we're staying where we are.

Leave the door unlocked

I don't believe we need to live in constant discomfort to grow.

I don't want that life.

I want places, people, routines, and experiences that feel familiar and safe. I need them.

What I don't want is to become so comfortable with the life I already know that I quietly stop considering the life I haven't discovered yet.

So I'll keep the comfortable chair.

I'll just try not to let it decide whether I ever get up.

A good place to rest doesn't have to become the place we stay.
